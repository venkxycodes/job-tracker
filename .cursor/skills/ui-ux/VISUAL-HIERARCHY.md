# Visual Hierarchy

Use when reviewing layout, grouping, spacing, emphasis, visual consistency, scannability, or whether design directs attention to the right thing.

## Core Principles

- Proximity: nearby items feel related.
- Common region: elements inside a shared boundary feel grouped.
- Similarity: similar-looking elements are read as related or equivalent.
- Uniform connectedness: visually connected elements feel more related than unconnected ones.
- Pragnanz: people interpret ambiguous visuals as the simplest plausible form.
- Selective attention: users focus on goal-relevant stimuli and miss noise.
- Von Restorff effect: the different item is remembered; use distinction sparingly.
- Aesthetic-usability effect: polished design can feel more usable, but polish cannot replace clarity.

## Agent Checks

1. Are related labels, controls, helper text, errors, and data visually grouped?
2. Are unrelated groups separated by spacing, boundaries, headings, or layout?
3. Do similar-looking controls behave similarly?
4. Is the most important content/action largest, highest contrast, or best positioned?
5. Is visual emphasis limited to elements that truly need attention?
6. Does the page have one clear reading path?
7. Are destructive/warning states distinct from normal states without relying on color alone?
8. Are dense dashboards summarized before details?
9. Do tables align numbers, labels, and actions for quick scanning?
10. Does responsive layout preserve grouping and priority?
11. Are decorative effects, motion, or illustrations competing with task completion?
12. Is the simplest interpretation of layout the intended interpretation?

## Common Failure Modes

- Card boundary groups unrelated controls because spacing was convenient.
- Primary and secondary actions share color, size, and position.
- Red, badges, animation, and bold all compete for attention.
- Table row action is far from row context.
- Mobile stacking destroys relationship between field, helper text, and error.
